AI search is hard to measure because the value arrives in three different places: visits you can attribute, fetches you can only see in server logs, and mentions that never produce a click at all. No single report covers all three. This guide shows what each method can and cannot see, so you can build a measurement setup that is honest about its blind spots.

If you are still working out whether AI engines mention you at all, start with the [ChatGPT visibility checker guide](/blog/chatgpt-visibility-checker) and come back here to set up ongoing tracking.

## The three things to measure

| Signal | What it tells you | Where to see it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AI referral visits | People clicked a link in an AI answer and reached your site | Analytics (GA4 or similar) |
| AI crawler requests | AI systems fetched your pages | Server or CDN logs |
| Citations and mentions | Answers named or linked your brand or URL | Manual or automated prompt tests |

Think of them as related but independent signals. Crawler requests show you are reachable, citations show you are chosen, and referral visits show the answer sent someone to you. They do not always move together: an engine can name a page it learned about through another provider or another page without any request from the AI user agents you monitor, so a quiet crawler log does not prove a citation is impossible.

## 1. Count AI referral visits in analytics

Visits from AI products arrive with a referrer or a tracking parameter. Common sources to look for:

- `chatgpt.com` (and the older `chat.openai.com`)
- `perplexity.ai`
- `gemini.google.com`
- `copilot.microsoft.com`
- `claude.ai`

ChatGPT also appends `utm_source=chatgpt.com` to many outbound links, which makes those visits easy to segment even when the referrer is stripped. Other products behave differently and change over time, so check your own data rather than assuming a fixed list.

In GA4, create a custom channel group or an exploration filtered on session source matching those domains. Keep the list in one place and review it each quarter, because new AI products and referrer formats appear regularly.

What this misses: anyone who read your brand in an answer and later typed your name into a search bar or visited directly. Expect AI-influenced demand to leak into branded search and direct traffic, so watch those channels for lift after you improve citations.

## 2. Estimate Google AI Overviews impact with proxies

Google now has a dedicated generative AI performance report in Search Console that shows impressions from AI Overviews and AI Mode. It appears only for sites with enough impressions in those features, and it is limited to Web search. Clicks are a different story: they are counted inside ordinary organic data, and in GA4 they appear as google / organic. You cannot filter them out, so combine the report with proxies:

- Open the generative AI report in Search Console and note which pages and queries earn impressions.
- Compare impressions and clicks for informational queries over time. Rising impressions with falling click-through rate on a page that answers a question is consistent with an AI answer taking the click, though not proof.
- Search the queries yourself and note whether an AI Overview appears and whether your page is listed in its sources.
- Annotate your reports with dates of site changes so you can separate your own edits from changes in the results page.

Our post on [how AI Overviews affect organic traffic](/blog/google-ai-overviews-organic-traffic) goes deeper on adapting to that shift.

## 3. Read server logs for AI crawler activity

Logs show something analytics cannot: whether AI systems fetch your pages at all. Filter your access logs or CDN analytics by user agent. The main ones to look for:

| User agent | Operator | What it usually means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| OAI-SearchBot | OpenAI | Indexing for ChatGPT search results |
| GPTBot | OpenAI | Collecting content for model training |
| ChatGPT-User | OpenAI | A person's request caused a fetch |
| PerplexityBot | Perplexity | Indexing for Perplexity answers |
| Perplexity-User | Perplexity | A person's request caused a fetch |
| Claude-SearchBot | Anthropic | Indexing for Claude search |
| ClaudeBot | Anthropic | Collecting content for model training |

Track requests per week for each agent, the URLs they fetch and the status codes returned. Three patterns are worth acting on:

1. **No requests at all** from a search crawler for weeks. Check robots.txt first, then firewall or bot-protection rules that may be blocking it before robots.txt is even read.
2. **Many 403 or 429 responses.** A security layer is rejecting the crawler. Allow-list the published crawler IP ranges if the operator provides them.
3. **Requests only for your homepage.** Internal links or your sitemap may not expose deeper pages.

User agent strings can be spoofed, so for decisions that matter, verify against the operator's published IP ranges. Our [robots.txt for AI crawlers](/blog/robots-txt-ai-crawlers) guide covers the allow-or-block choice, and the free [AI crawler checker](/tools/ai-crawler-checker) shows how your robots.txt treats each one today.

## 4. Test citations with a fixed prompt set

Citations are the signal closest to the outcome you want, and the only way to measure them is to ask. Build a small, stable set of prompts:

1. Write 10 to 20 questions your buyers actually ask, mixing category questions ("best payroll software for small teams") and problem questions ("how do I run payroll in two states").
2. Run each prompt in the engines your audience uses, such as ChatGPT with search, Perplexity and Google.
3. Record, for every run, whether your brand is named, whether your URL is cited and which competitors appear.
4. Repeat each prompt a few times, because answers vary between runs, and report a rate (for example, named in 3 of 5 runs) rather than a yes or no.
5. Keep the prompt wording fixed so week-over-week changes mean something.

A spreadsheet is enough to start. Log date, engine, prompt, brand mentioned, URL cited and competitors named. After a month you will see which topics you own, which belong to someone else and which no one answers well. Those gaps are your content plan. The step-by-step method is in [how to check if your website shows up in ChatGPT and Perplexity](/blog/how-to-check-website-visible-chatgpt-perplexity).

## 5. Track the inputs you control

Citations and traffic are outcomes. Some inputs are measurable on every page and tell you early whether your work is moving the right way:

- Can AI crawlers fetch the page, and is the content present in the HTML?
- Are metadata, canonical URL and structured data correct?
- Do sections open with a direct, quotable answer?

## A simple weekly routine

1. Check AI referral sessions and top landing pages in analytics.
2. Check crawler requests, errors and newly fetched URLs in your logs.
3. Run your prompt set and update the citation log.
4. Re-scan pages you changed and note the score difference.
5. Write down one change to make next week, and the date, so later swings are explainable.

## Limits to keep in mind

- AI products change referrer behavior, link formats and crawler names without notice. Recheck your filters quarterly.
- Answers are not deterministic, so a single prompt run proves little. Rates over several runs are more reliable.
- Many mentions never produce a tracked click. Judge success by a combination of citations, referral visits and branded demand, not by referral sessions alone.
- None of these numbers prove causation. Pair them with dated change notes.
